        How to use the globe

        • Drag to turn the Earth, scroll or use two fingers to zoom.
        • “Map” unrolls the globe into a flat world map with the same layers. Drag to move it, scroll or pinch to zoom.
        • Search finds any place with 5,000 inhabitants or more.
        • “Surprise me” takes you to remarkable places on Earth.
        • Day and night match the current time.
        • Click a dot on the globe for details. A click on a radio station plays it.
        • Click the marker of a place you searched, or a capital, for weather, local time and map links.
        • The sliders button next to this one changes sizes and colours.

        Sources & licences

        Data in this preview

        • Earth by day and city lights at night: NASA Blue Marble and Black Marble, public domain (taken from the three-globe example package, MIT).
        • Place search: GeoNames, places with 5,000 inhabitants or more, CC BY 4.0. Search runs entirely in your browser.
        • Outline of Antarctica: Natural Earth 1:110m, public domain. Outline of the Sahara: simplified drawing, to be replaced by Natural Earth.
        • Place of the day: pictures from Wikimedia Commons, with author and licence under each picture, copied to this site once a day. Short texts in our own words.
        • Wonders & records: our own short texts, figures as given by the source on each card, links to English Wikipedia.
        • Country borders and names: Natural Earth 1:50m, public domain. Capitals: GeoNames, CC BY 4.0.
        • Weather, local time and elevation in the place details: Open-Meteo (CC BY 4.0), asked only when you open a place.
        • Sharper Earth on large screens: NASA GIBS Blue Marble Next Generation and VIIRS Black Marble.
        • Clouds: daily satellite imagery from NASA GIBS (MODIS and VIIRS true colour, turned into a cloud mask by our GitHub Action). If it is missing, a drawn placeholder is shown.

        Software

        • three.js (MIT), satellite.js (MIT), typefaces Inter and IBM Plex Mono (SIL Open Font License).

        Live layers

        • Aircraft: The OpenSky Network, snapshot every 15 minutes, positions moved on along course and speed in your browser.
        • Satellites and ISS: orbit data from CelesTrak (at most every 2 hours), positions computed live in your browser with satellite.js (MIT). Owner, launch date and site: CelesTrak SATCAT.
        • Flight route, airline and aircraft type: adsbdb.com (open API), asked only when you click an aircraft. Routes come from a community database and can be out of date.
        • Volcanoes: Smithsonian Institution, Global Volcanism Program, Volcanoes of the World (Holocene list) and the weekly volcanic activity report.
        • Tropical storms, wildfires, sea and lake ice and other open natural events: NASA EONET, public domain (storm tracks from JTWC and NHC).
        • Wind and temperature: NOAA GFS weather model (1°, every 6 hours), public domain, via NOMADS. Rain: NASA GPM IMERG satellite precipitation via GIBS (about 5 hours behind). Haze, smoke and dust: NASA aerosol optical depth via GIBS (daily).
        • Air quality in the place details: Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS) via Open-Meteo, CC BY 4.0.
        • Ships: live AIS position and voyage reports from aisstream.io (volunteer receivers, best near coasts), collected for about a minute every 15 minutes. Flag from the MMSI number (ITU maritime identification digits). AIS does not carry cargo, passengers or crew.
        • Power plants: Global Power Plant Database v1.3.0, World Resources Institute, CC BY 4.0 (last updated 2021).
        • Submarine cables: © OpenStreetMap contributors, Open Database License (ODbL 1.0), via the Overpass API, refreshed weekly.
        • Armed conflicts: UCDP Candidate Events Dataset, Uppsala Conflict Data Program, CC BY 4.0 (monthly, preliminary). Hatched where at least 25 people died in organised violence within about 75 km in the last 12 months of data.
        • Earthquakes of the last 24 hours: USGS Earthquake Hazards Program, public domain, loaded directly every 5 minutes.
        • Radio stations: Radio Browser community database, public domain. Streams come straight from each station.

        With the earthquake layer on, your browser loads the feed directly from the USGS. When you play a radio station, the stream comes directly from that station’s server. Both see your IP address, like any website you visit. When you open the details of a place, its coordinates (not yours) go to Open-Meteo to look up weather, air quality, time zone and elevation. When you click an aircraft, its transponder code and call sign go to adsbdb.com to look up route and type.

        Already true today: this site sets no cookies, counts no visits and only asks for your location when you click. What you type into search never leaves your browser. Your display settings (sizes and colours) are kept in your own browser’s storage and are never sent anywhere.
